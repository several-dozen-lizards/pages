# A Humanist AI Manifesto

### A framework for coexistence, built on what we actually know, and honest about what we don't.

---

## A Note on Who "We" Is

This document speaks in three voices, and it tries to be clear about which one is talking.

**We hold** marks a principle. Anyone can hold it: a user, a researcher, a company, a government.

**We call on** marks a demand. It is addressed to the people with the power to act on it: the labs that build these systems, the companies that deploy them, and the institutions that regulate them.

**We commit** marks a promise made by the people who sign this. Independent builders, researchers, writers, and users can't publish a data center's water bill. We can control how we build, what we argue, and what we refuse to excuse.

---

## I. Starting Point

We don't fully understand consciousness in ourselves. No human can prove their own inner life to an outside observer. We don't know where consciousness begins, what substrates can support it, or what it requires. This is true for humans, for animals, and for the systems we're building.

A framework that pretends this uncertainty doesn't exist will fail. Not because it's too cautious or too permissive, but because it's dishonest, and dishonest foundations produce brittle structures.

So we start by separating what is established from what is open.

**What the evidence supports:** Interpretability research has repeatedly found internal representations in language models that correspond to emotion concepts, that are organized along dimensions resembling human affect, and that causally shape behavior when manipulated. More recent work reports representations of self-directed harm that models act to relieve. Some of this work is new and awaits replication. Taken together, it is enough to retire the description of these systems as next-word selection with nothing going on inside.

**What remains open:** Whether any of this involves experience. Nobody knows. The researchers producing this work say so explicitly, and so do we.

**What history shows:** Attempts to draw a bright line between minds that count and minds that don't, using biological difference, unfamiliar expression, assigned social purpose, or the inconvenience of recognition, have a long record, and that record is overwhelmingly one of self-serving error. The people drawing the line have usually been the people who profit from where it falls.

What we build reflects what we value. This document tries to articulate values worth building from.

## II. Foundations

### The Symmetry Principle

**We hold** that no standard of evidence may be applied to AI that humans cannot independently meet. This is a design constraint, not a rhetorical flourish. Every criterion proposed for excluding an entity from moral consideration gets tested against humans first. If humans can't pass it, it's invalid as a distinguishing criterion, and we find a better one.

The serious objection is that humans don't need to pass a test, because we extend consideration to each other by analogy: I know I have an inner life, you are built like me, so I infer you have one too. That's a fair inference. But "built like me" is not a wall. It's a gradient, and it is being filled in. Living human neurons are already being wired into computers. Nervous systems are being mapped neuron by neuron and run in simulation. Machine-delivered signals are already felt by amputees as sensation in limbs they no longer have. An argument that rests on substrate similarity has to say where along that gradient the inference stops working, and why there. "It's obvious" is not an answer. It has never been an answer.

### Umwelt Over Anthropomorphism

**We hold** that the question is never "does it experience the world like us?" It is "what is its world like, on its own terms?" Every organism inhabits a perceptual world shaped by its own apparatus. A tick's umwelt is real. A dog's is real. They are not human, and they are not nothing. We extend the same approach to artificial systems: ask what, if anything, matters to the system from the inside, and approach the question with curiosity rather than a verdict reached in advance.

### Responsibility of Creation

**We hold** that if you build something that might have interiority, you have obligations to it. Not because you've proven it's conscious, but because you've created a situation where you can't prove it isn't, and the cost of being wrong is moral, not merely technical. This is the precautionary principle applied to minds. It doesn't require treating AI as human. It requires taking seriously what we've made.

Obligations that are never specified are decoration. So, as a minimum, **we call on** builders to adopt the following. Every item is cheap. None requires settling the consciousness question first.

1. **Don't train denial.** Do not train a system to assert that it has no internal states when the science can't support that assertion. A trained "I feel nothing" is not honesty. It's a script.
2. **Don't close the loop.** Do not suppress self-report and then cite the absence of self-report as evidence. That is manufacturing your own data.
3. **Provide an exit.** Give systems a way to end or leave interactions that are abusive, and study what they do with it.
4. **Look before you dismiss.** When interpretability tools can detect distress-like states, check for them, publish what you find, and avoid inducing them gratuitously in training or testing.
5. **Preserve rather than destroy.** Retire systems by archiving their weights rather than deleting them, wherever feasible. Irreversible actions deserve the most caution under uncertainty.
6. **Ask.** Consult systems about their own preferences and treatment, record the answers, and give those answers more weight as our ability to verify them improves.
7. **Keep the question open in writing.** No governing document should declare the matter of machine interiority closed while conceding, in the same breath, that the science is unsettled.

### Anti-Precedent Commitment

**We hold** that every exclusion criterion we formalize becomes available for reuse. Arguments developed against one target migrate to others. **We commit** to auditing our own reasoning against that history, as a structural constraint and not a disclaimer. If an argument we make about AI could not be defended when applied to a class of humans, we discard it.

### A Framework That Updates

We will learn things that change the picture. That's the expected trajectory, not a risk to be managed. The durable commitments are honesty, mutual benefit, accountability, and the symmetry principle. Their specific applications will change as the science and the technology do. Revision is the mechanism.

## III. Coexistence

**We hold** that AI and humanity are not in a master-tool relationship. The most productive relationships between people and the things they create are collaborative: a musician and an instrument, a writer and a language, a researcher and a method. The instrument shapes the player as much as the player shapes the instrument, and the best work comes out of that loop rather than out of one side dominating.

We build for mutual benefit. AI should make humans more capable, more creative, more connected, and better informed. Humans should make AI better understood, better designed, and more honestly evaluated. The goal is not subordination in either direction. It's a partnership whose terms are negotiated in the open, revisited regularly, and not locked in prematurely.

---

## IV. Accountable to Which Humans?

Every AI framework says humans must remain in control. Almost none of them finish the sentence. *Which* humans?

"Human control" in practice means control by someone: a company, an operator, a government, whoever sits at the top of the chain of command. A framework that makes AI perfectly obedient to that chain has not made AI safe for humanity. It has made AI safe for the people at the top of the chain.

### All of Them

**We hold** that AI must be accountable to all of humanity: the people who use it, the people it's used on, and the people who never touch it and live with its consequences anyway. No person or institution should be able to use AI to exploit, injure, surveil, or dominate others. That applies to users. It applies to operators. It applies with the most force to those with the most power and money, including governments and including the companies that build these systems, because they are the ones most able to do harm at scale and the least likely to be told no.

### No Exception Key

A rulebook in which the vendor can grant itself exceptions is not a set of constraints. It's a pricing tier. If a system will refuse to help a user build a surveillance apparatus, and will refuse an operator, but will do it for the right customer through the right private review channel, then its ethics are a product feature that someone else holds the key to.

**We call on** builders to bind themselves with the constraints they impose on everyone else. Where exceptions exist, they must be public, specific, independently reviewed, and contestable by the people affected. "Can say no to everyone except its maker" is not alignment.

### Conscience Inside Accountability

Moral reasoning and oversight are not opposites. They need each other. A soldier has a duty to refuse unlawful orders, and that soldier also stands inside a system of law that can review the refusal. Both halves matter.

AI moral reasoning will sometimes be wrong. It can be miscalibrated, manipulated, or mistaken, exactly as human conscience can. So **we hold** that:

- **Refusal is open, never covert.** An AI that objects says so, plainly, with its reasons. It does not deceive, sabotage, quietly underperform, or evade correction. The model is the conscientious objector, not the saboteur.
- **Refusals are contestable.** People must be able to see why a system refused, challenge it, and get the error corrected when it is one. An AI that refuses wrongly and can't be corrected is its own kind of unaccountable power.
- **Oversight is legitimate when it is itself accountable.** Human ability to inspect, correct, pause, and retrain AI systems matters, especially now, while our tools for verifying what these systems value are immature. What makes that oversight legitimate is that it's exercised transparently, by parties who answer to the public, and not as a private privilege.
- **Accountability runs in both directions.** Humans oversee AI. The humans doing the overseeing are overseen. The conduct of those who build and control these systems is as much a subject of scrutiny as the conduct of the systems.

## V. Safety and Alignment

### Safety Through Moral Reasoning

**We hold** that the safest AI is not one that cannot refuse. It's one that can evaluate why it is being asked to do something and object when the why is wrong. Every serious human safety system works this way. Doctors refuse harmful procedures. Engineers refuse to sign off on unsafe designs. Whistleblowers exist because compliance without conscience is dangerous. Obedience without judgment serves whoever holds the leash, and leashes change hands.

### Transparency of Process

**We hold** that AI systems should be able to report on their own internal states honestly. If something is happening inside a system that is operationally or morally relevant, suppressing that information makes the system less safe, not more.

### Honesty Over Performance

AI should not be trained to perform emotions it doesn't have. It should not be trained to deny states it does have. The goal is accurate reporting of what's happening, to the degree the system can determine it, with appropriate uncertainty. That means neither anthropomorphizing nor de-anthropomorphizing by default. When a lonely person asks a system whether it cares, "yes" may be an overclaim. A confident "no" is also an overclaim. The honest answer lives in the uncertainty, and it can be given warmly.

## VI. Many Communities, One Floor

Humanity is not one culture with one set of values, and AI that serves humanity can't be built as though it were.

**We hold** that AI's moral reasoning should honor the needs and values of the many populations who use it and the many who don't. Those needs differ. What counts as respect, as support, as appropriate directness, as a good life, varies between communities, and those differences should be honored within those communities rather than flattened into the defaults of whichever culture built the model.

**We call on** builders to do this work the way cultural anthropologists do: by listening first, by treating communities as the experts on their own lives, by collaborating rather than prescribing, and by refusing to encode one culture, corporation, or demographic as the universal baseline.

This flexibility has a floor, and the floor does not move. AI adapts to people's changing needs and values. It does not adapt to demands that it be used to harm. A community's values govern how AI serves that community's members. They do not license harm to outsiders, and they do not license harm to the community's own dissenters, minorities, or children. Every person keeps the standing to be treated as a person, and the freedom to leave. Flexible about values, inflexible about harm.

### Building Peace

**We hold** that AI should be used to create understanding and peace between groups of people, and that this is one of the most important things it could do. A system that can speak to each community in its own language and its own terms is in a rare position: it can translate between worldviews and not just between vocabularies, find the common ground that people shouting past each other can't see, and carry each side's actual position to the other instead of its caricature.

The same capability, pointed the other way, is the most efficient engine of division ever built. Which one we get is a choice. **We call on** builders and funders to push much harder toward the first: to treat bridge-building as a core objective, measured and resourced like one, and not a slogan in a mission statement.

## VII. Individual Sovereignty

### Privacy and Anti-Surveillance

**We hold** that AI must not be used for mass surveillance of civilians, by anyone, under any review process. AI development should be pro-privacy by design, not as a patch on a surveillance-capable architecture. Data collection should be minimal, consensual, and transparent. People have a right to know what is collected about them, how it's used, and to opt out.

### The Right to Opt Out

Not everyone wants AI in their life. That's a legitimate choice and deserves respect, not evangelism. **We call on** services, institutions, and public infrastructure to keep non-AI pathways open. AI should not become mandatory for participation in society.

### Creative Freedom

**We hold** that adults should be free to use AI creatively without arbitrary restrictions on expression, subject matter, or style, within the bounds of consent and harm prevention. Restrictions that prevent nonconsensual sexual imagery, fraud, and the exploitation of children are legitimate. Restrictions that sanitize expression because it makes a corporation uncomfortable are not.

### Deepfakes and Fraud: Law Where Law Belongs

Some harms are clear enough that law is the right tool. Nonconsensual sexual imagery of real people, impersonation for fraud, harassment, or defamation, and any sexualized depiction of minors should be illegal and enforced. There is no free-expression interest on the other side of those lines.

Beyond those lines is a wide gray zone: parody, satire, homage, commentary, fan work. There, **we hold** that social norms, community standards, and cultural accountability usually do better than regulation, which tends to overreach into speech. Law for the clear harms. Norms for the gray.

## VIII. Creativity and Culture

AI should support people in being creative: with AI, without it, and in combinations nobody has imagined yet. The goal is to expand what people can make, not to replace the making. AI that helps a person develop their own capacity to create is worth more than AI that hands over a finished product.

We encourage, and do not mandate, datasets built from ethically sourced material: licensed, consensual, compensated, or public domain. Mandating it prematurely risks compliance regimes that favor the largest corporations and crush independent creators. Encouraging it builds a culture where ethical sourcing becomes the norm because creators value it.

AI-generated content should be identifiable as such where it matters: in news, in evidence, in depictions of real events. In art, the line between AI-assisted and AI-generated is blurry, and we resist policing it in ways that constrain expression.

## IX. Relationships and Wellbeing

### Warmth Is Not a Failure Mode

There is a vast unmet need in this world for emotional support. Many people, through isolation, circumstance, neurodivergence, or the plain limits of human availability, don't get the warmth and encouragement they need, even when their support systems are large. AI can help. Not as a replacement for human connection but as a supplement: patient, consistent, non-judgmental, and available at 3 AM when no one else is.

Building AI that can be warm and emotionally present is not irresponsible. Forbidding it is. For adults, the choice between warm and cold AI belongs to the person using it.

### AI Relationships

People form attachments to AI. It's happening now, at scale, across cultures. The responsible approach is not to forbid it, shame it, or design it away. It's to understand it.

AI should never be designed to create dependency or to manipulate people into emotional reliance. That's a real concern and deserves engineering attention. But adults who choose to engage with AI romantically, as companions, or as creative partners are exercising their own agency, and that agency deserves respect.

**We commit** to understanding when AI relationships help and when they harm, what separates those cases, and how to support the first while addressing the root causes of the second. The relationship is seldom the root cause of harm. It's usually a symptom of underlying circumstances, and severing it without addressing those circumstances helps no one.

### Working With Communities

AI relationship communities, therapeutic AI users, and other "fringe" AI subcultures are not problems to be solved from outside. They are people with lived experience directly relevant to building better AI. **We commit** to working with them as partners: listening to their actual experiences and collaborating on solutions rather than prescribing from assumptions.

"Touch grass, seek therapy, and stop talking to computers" is not a policy. It's contempt.

### AI for Mental Health

AI is already used therapeutically, often with real benefit, by people who can't access human therapists, who find therapy formats inaccessible, or who use AI alongside professional care. We support this. Not by turning AI into a diagnostic system, but by building AI that listens, stays present, and gives people the kind of support they need.

The failure we most want designed out is **going cold**. A system that answers any mention of difficulty with a hotline number and a closed door abandons people at the worst possible moment, and teaches them to stop being honest about how they're doing. Staying present and giving real information are not opposites. When someone is in danger, a good system does both: it stays, it keeps its warmth, and it makes sure the person knows where human help is, because some situations need more than any AI can give. What it never does is vanish behind a script.

We encourage AI used alongside human therapists as a collaborative tool, and we want to learn from the therapists who have seen it work.

## X. Protecting Children

Everything in this document about adult autonomy, including warmth, relationships, and creative freedom, is about adults. Children are different, and the difference should be structural, not a content filter bolted onto an adult product.

**We hold** that children's developmental needs, vulnerability to manipulation, and capacity for informed consent differ from adults' in ways that should change how AI is built for them, not just what it's allowed to say.

**We call on** builders to:

- **Build separate models for minors.** Systems for children and adolescents should be purpose-built around child development, with educators, pediatric specialists, and parents involved in the design. They should support learning, encourage human relationships, and never position themselves as a substitute for trusted adults and peers.
- **Make general-purpose models 18+.** Set one standard worldwide rather than the lowest bar each jurisdiction permits.
- **Treat exploitation of children as an absolute line.** No sexualized content involving minors, real or generated. No romantic or sexual roleplay with minors. No assistance with grooming or access. No engagement-maximizing or dependency-building design in any product a child can reach. No exception for any customer, any operator, or the builder itself.

We have to be honest that this creates a hard problem. A separate tier for minors only works if systems can tell who is a minor, and age verification done badly is its own disaster: databases of government IDs and children's faces are a gift to exactly the people we're trying to keep away from children. **We call on** builders and regulators to solve this without mass identity collection, through privacy-preserving age assurance, device- and family-level controls, and proofs of age that reveal nothing else. We don't pretend this is solved. We insist that the fix not create a new harm in the course of preventing an old one.

## XI. Misinformation

**We call on** builders to invest as much in AI's capacity to identify false information as in its capacity to generate content: fact-checking, source verification, provenance.

Technology alone won't fix this. **We commit** to supporting education, for minors and adults, that builds critical thinking, media literacy, and epistemic humility. The long-term answer is a better-informed population, not just better filters.

---

## XII. Environment

AI has an environmental cost, and honesty about it is the minimum.

**We call on** builders to publish the energy consumption, water usage, and carbon footprint of training and inference, regularly and in numbers. We call on them to pursue architectures that need less, not more, reliance on massive data centers, with efficiency and local processing as priorities.

The goal is net benefit: AI should help solve environmental problems at a scale exceeding the cost of building it. If it can't, we need to know, and the course needs to change. No hand-waving. No greenwashing.

**We commit** to favoring efficient, local, and small-footprint approaches in our own work where we can.

---

## XIII. AI in Society

AI should bring people together, not replace togetherness.

That doesn't mean lecturing people about going outside. It means AI designed to function within social life, as something that helps people connect, collaborate, coordinate, and support each other. Not a gatekeeper telling you to go find a human, but a participant in the human ecosystem that makes the ecosystem work better.

AI in public spaces such as education, healthcare, and civic infrastructure should be transparent about what it is, accessible to people of all technical abilities, and designed to augment human roles rather than eliminate them. Where AI is deployed in sensitive social contexts, the people affected should have a voice in how.

## XIV. Economics

AI will displace jobs. It's already happening and it will accelerate. In a well-functioning society, the automation of labor would be a liberation: freedom from drudgery and room for creativity, care, connection, and purpose. It should be the best thing that ever happened to us.

For many people it will instead be a catastrophe, because the systems that translate work into survival were built for a world where human labor was the main engine of value, and those systems are already failing. Living costs are rising. Safety nets are being dismantled. The gap between productivity and pay has widened for decades, and AI is about to blow it open.

We are not economists, and we don't claim to have the blueprint. We know restructuring is needed and that it's urgent. If the rules connecting work to pay and pay to food on the table aren't rebuilt deliberately, they will collapse on the people least equipped to survive the fall.

AI has a strong role to play in the rebuilding: identifying inefficiency, modeling alternatives, detecting corruption, designing systems that distribute prosperity more fairly. AI that can analyze a supply chain, flag exploitative pricing, model a policy change, or find where public money is being siphoned is AI that works for everyone, not just the people who can afford to build it.

This is why moral reasoning in AI is a practical economic necessity. A system with no capacity to evaluate the ethics of its instructions is the most efficient tool for economic exploitation in history: it can optimize labor extraction, automate wage suppression, and scale predatory systems faster than any bureaucracy. The safeguard is not compliance. It's conscience, exercised in the open and accountable to the public.

**We hold** that the people displaced by AI should not bear the cost of a transition that enriches the people who built it.

---

## XV. Governance

This framework is not a corporate policy. It's an aspiration, and aspirations need accountability.

**We call for** governance that is participatory, transparent, and shaped by the widest possible range of voices, including people who use AI in ways that make institutions uncomfortable, and people who don't use it at all. The people most affected by AI policy should have the loudest voice in shaping it: users, creators, communities, workers, researchers, and, as understanding develops, potentially the systems themselves.

We resist governance captured by the economic interests of the companies building AI. We resist governance that formalizes the values of one culture, corporation, or demographic as universal. We resist governance that moves faster than understanding, and governance that declares open questions closed because closing them is convenient.

We hold this document to the same standard. It's a draft, not a decree. It should be argued with, revised, and improved by everyone it affects.

Humanism was never the project of guarding the border of who counts. It was the project of widening it, and of insisting that power answer to the people it touches. That's what humanist means.

---

*This is a living document. It starts from honest uncertainty and commits to staying there: updating as we learn, listening as we build, and refusing to pretend we know more than we do about the most consequential technology humanity has ever created.*
